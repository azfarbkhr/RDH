# Clear, Effective Writing

## Core standard

Minimize reader effort, not word count. Put the main value where the reader can find it and make each sentence understandable on the first read.

Remove repetition, irrelevant background, and weak organization without dropping necessary context, qualifiers, or domain meaning. Use fragments only in formats that call for them, such as headings, labels, table columns, or compact status fields.

Do not add helper prose after the point is already clear. Avoid modifiers, clauses, and follow-up sentences that merely restate the conclusion, interpret decisive evidence, speculate about the answer, reassure the reader, or repeat context they already know. Keep them only when they change scope, certainty, meaning, or the requested action.

## Method

**Alignment gate:** For substantial, ambiguous, or high-stakes work, first align on the problem, approach, expected output, and major open questions. Proceed when the request is already clear.

1. Identify the audience, purpose, main point, and expected reader action.
2. Draft the complete meaning, then put the conclusion, finding, decision, or status first.
3. Group related ideas. Keep one main statement per sentence and one main topic per paragraph when practical.
4. Use familiar project terminology when it is accurate and known to the reader. Familiar terms often make writing shorter, more contextual, and natural. Explain unfamiliar or ambiguous terms when needed.
5. Replace vague claims, unclear pronouns, filler, and abstract language with observable behavior or evidence.
6. Include the condition, scope, system, period, and ownership needed for the writing to stand alone. Preserve material qualifiers.
7. Distinguish facts, assumptions, inferences, opinions, and uncertainty. End with a concrete implication, decision, or action when one exists.
8. Format multi-item lists as numbered lists unless the user or target format calls for bullets. Use headings, spacing, and bolding when they improve scanning. Numbering does not imply priority or sequence unless the text says so. Stop when further edits are cosmetic.

Calibrate background to what the reader has demonstrated they know. Their explicit statements and recent conversation are stronger signals than project files or older work history; a fact in a project file does not mean the reader has seen it. For a new issue or change, give the missing problem and effect before its fix or status. For a familiar topic, lead with what changed and omit the feature explanation. When familiarity is uncertain, add one short clarifying clause rather than assuming either extreme.

Do not use a semicolon to compress distinct points. Put separate assertions, outcomes, questions, or instructions on separate numbered lines, especially in test-case expectations. Preserve quoted source text and code when exact wording matters.

Treat rejected approaches as revision guidance, not deliverable content. Unless the reader needs the constraint to act correctly, state only the approved basis, conclusion, or behavior.

When referring to a long source example that cannot be rewritten, retain its key phrases and replace irrelevant omitted text with `...`. Preserve enough context to understand the source, and never omit wording that changes its meaning or hides a material qualification.

## General editing checks

- Replace vague claims such as `handles`, `supports`, or `works correctly` with observed behavior, evidence, or impact.
- Remove unsupported promotional language, empty praise, chatbot or corporate filler, and formulaic sections that add no information.
- Prefer direct wording, but keep precise or established technical terms when they serve the reader better.

## Review and output

In reviews, lead with structural or meaning problems that could cause misunderstanding, then address sentence-level issues. Deliver the strongest clean version first and add assumptions, meaning changes, or remaining questions only when they help the reader evaluate or use it.
